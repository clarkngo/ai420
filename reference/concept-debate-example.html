The setup

HOS 1 · Week 1O1

The question (already in the Module 1 lesson): For a multi-step business task, should you build an agent that decides its own steps, or a fixed workflow that calls the model only where needed?

  • Side A — Let an agent decide the steps. Give the model tools and a goal; let it choose the path.
  • Side B — A fixed workflow with LLM steps. Hard-code the steps; use the model only for the parts that need language.

Before the first debate, the class is split into two debate groups of about the same size, either assigned by the instructor or chosen by students. In HOS 1, Debate Group 1 argues Side A and Debate Group 2 argues Side B. The four students below are fictional.

Timeline

Week 1 of the HOSInstructor steps in purple

Days follow the suggested pacing in the lessons; your Brightspace dates win.

Week 1 · Monday

The topic opens

After the lecture, students read the question and both sides in the lesson. The instructor posts nothing new; the question is already in the course.

Monday · instructor

Check the groups

Confirm the two debate groups are close to even, and place anyone who added late.

Monday – Tuesday

Stage 1: initial position

Each student starts their own thread, titled with their side and name. Until they post it, Brightspace hides everyone else's threads.

Wednesday · instructor

Skim Stage 1 and nudge

About 15 minutes. Look for topic summaries with no claim or evidence, and leave one short public question on those threads. Note anyone who hasn't posted.

Wednesday – Thursday

Stage 2: argue the opposite

Each student replies to their own thread with the strongest case for the side they didn't argue. Now they can read everyone's posts.

Friday – Saturday

Stage 3: synthesis

Each student replies to a classmate's thread from the other side, citing one specific point from it. The topic closes Saturday night.

Week 2 · Monday · instructor

Grade and reuse

Grade from the topic's Assessment view (about 2 minutes per student; each student's posts appear together). Quote one strong synthesis, anonymized or with permission, in the Week 2 lecture.

Stage 1 · Monday – Tuesday: initial position

Side A — PriyaSide AStage 1Mon 8:40 pm

Claim: an agent should decide the steps, because a business task's steps depend on what the earlier steps find.

Evidence: in the Agent Loop Simulator, the Mount Rainier question took a search, two lookups and a calculation, and the agent chose that order from what each result said. A fixed workflow would need a branch for every way a lookup can come back. Adding a capability to the agent is one tool, not a rewrite of the control flow.

Why it works: one claim, and evidence from something she actually ran.
Side B — DanaSide BStage 1Tue 10:15 am

Claim: a fixed workflow is the better default, because most of a business task doesn't need deciding.

Evidence: in my HOS 1 run of compare.py, the ADK agent used about four times the tokens of the single call and took several times as long. A script that fetches the two facts and subtracts them costs one model call to phrase the answer, and it takes the same path every time, so I can test it.

Why it works: a real number from her own build, not a general worry.
Side A — MarcusSide AStage 1Tue 9:50 pm

A ReAct agent reasons, acts and observes in a loop until the task is done. It can use tools and remember earlier steps. This is the modern approach to AI and it's more flexible than a workflow.

What's missing: this is a summary of how it works, not an argument for why it's better. No specific claim, no evidence.
InstructorInstructorWed 9:10 am

Marcus, this explains what a ReAct agent does but not why it's the better choice for a business task. For Stage 2, think of one task where an agent's freedom would cost more than it's worth, and argue from that.

Instructor move: one public question, not a grade. Other students can see what a stronger post needs.
Side B — JordanSide BStage 1Wed 7:30 am

Claim: a fixed workflow is safer, because you can't audit a path you didn't design.

Evidence: the lecture's step-budget example. An agent that's allowed to choose its steps needs a cap in case it keeps going, and when it stops, someone has to work out why it went that way. A workflow fails at a named step.

Strong, but late: posted a day after the Stage 1 deadline. That costs Participation, not Writing.

Stage 2 · Wednesday – Thursday: argue the opposite (a reply to your own thread)

PriyaSide AStage 2Wed 6:05 pm

The strongest case for a fixed workflow: an agent's path isn't repeatable. The same request can take two routes, so I can't write a test that guarantees the expensive step only runs when it should. A workflow is boring, but boring is testable, and for a weekly report that runs the same way every Monday, testable beats flexible.

DanaSide BStage 2Thu 2:30 pm

The strongest case for an agent: workflows break on the first case nobody planned. A customer email can ask three things at once, or none of the things your branches cover. Every miss means another branch, until the workflow is a second, worse agent written by hand. And when a read-only step goes wrong, it costs a few tokens, not money.

MarcusSide AStage 2Thu 11:05 pm

The other side would say that a workflow is more predictable and cheaper.

JordanSide BStage 2Thu 8:00 pm

The strongest case for an agent: Priya's simulator run shows the next step really can depend on the last result, and a branch for every result isn't realistic. Auditing is a real concern, but it's a concern about what gets recorded, not about who picks the step. A logged agent run shows every decision it made.

Priya → Dana's threadSide AStage 3Fri 7:45 pm

Dana's token count changed where I draw the line. The agent was right on those questions, but a two-step script would have been right too, for a quarter of the cost. My position: use a fixed workflow when the steps are known in advance, and give a step to an agent only when what it needs to do depends on what it finds.

I'd keep the workflow as the outer shape: fixed steps, with an agent inside the one step whose work really varies. That keeps Priya's flexibility where it earns its cost, and the rest stays testable.

Set it up in Brightspace

Instructor · once per term, then once per debate
  1. One forum, "Concept Debates", with one topic per debate: HOS 1–4 and the Week 8 Integration debate. Paste the question and both sides into each topic's description.
  2. Turn on "Users must start a thread before they can read and reply to other threads in this topic" (Post and View settings). Nobody can read and borrow arguments before posting their own Stage 1.
  3. Set the topic's end date to the Stage 3 deadline. Brightspace allows one end date per topic, so check Stage 1 and Stage 2 times when grading rather than splitting a debate into three topics.
  4. Assessment tab: score out of 10 and attach the Participation 50 / Writing 50 rubric, so you can grade from the topic with each student's posts together.
  5. Tell students the thread title format: "Side A — First name L." It makes the topic easy to scan.
  6. Keep it one whole-class topic. Above about 30–35 students, use Brightspace groups to make two or three restricted copies of the topic, each with a mix of both sides. Don't split by side: Stages 2 and 3 need the other side's posts.

Not one thread for everyone: replies nest until they're unreadable, the start-a-thread-first setting stops working, and grading means hunting for each student's posts.
