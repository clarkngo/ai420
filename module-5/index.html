Term Project: Plan, Build & Present

All four HOS, combined into one multi-agent system that automates a real, multi-step business process, built on your own or in a group of up to three. What Weeks 9–10 are for

20% of courseOutcomes O1–O5Solo or group

The Term Project combines all 4 HOS — the agent loop and its patterns, custom tools over MCP with memory, traces and self-correction, and a team of agents with an evaluation — into one system that automates a real, multi-step business process from a single trigger. You've built every piece once already. The work now is synthesis: deciding how the pieces fit, building the combined version, and proving how well it works.

The course description's two examples both work as starting points: an autonomous marketing department (research → draft → brand review → schedule) or a self-healing coding assistant (read failing test → diagnose → patch → re-run). So does any real process that meets the minimum bar.

The project runs all term in three phases. By the end of Week 7 you have locked your scope (Phase 1) and have a rough working version (Phase 2). Phase 3 starts with the Week 8 architecture review, then a backlog in Week 9, then builds. Week 10 finishes, freezes, rehearses, and presents.

Day 1: map the manual process

The agentic mindset

Shadow or interview someone who does the process by hand, and list its steps. For each step, decide what it should be, and why. Human checkpoints are allowed only where a task truly needs one, and each needs a justification.

A step is…WhenExample, from promoting a campus event
An agentIt needs judgment, or its inputs vary.Research the audience and draft the post.
Deterministic codeA fixed rule does the job.Post at 9 a.m. the Tuesday before the event.
A human checkpointA decision truly needs a person.Approve anything that mentions a price or a named speaker.

It's the Module 1 question again, now for your own process: does this step need an agent at all?

🗂️

Backlog and Sprint 1

Scrum-liteKeep it brief

Turn each step of your process map into a user story — "As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]" — and tag each with the HOS it depends on. Then mark a small, realistic subset as Sprint 1: what you can actually finish in the rest of Week 9 and Week 10. Everything else stays backlog, not a promise. Your list stays in this browser; copy it into your PM tool when it's ready.

Depends on:

Coverage

Sprint 1 should touch at least two HOS end to end this week, and all four by the Week 10 demo.

Litmus test for each HOS

For every HOS, can you point to a specific part of your project and say why it's really that HOS? "This is HOS 2, and it's a real system the process touches, served over MCP — not a hardcoded list." If a HOS only has a token mention, the Architecture criterion and your final report will show it.

Roles and a project management tool

Day 1–2

Working solo? You own every area, so use the split below as a checklist and plan your sprints to cover each one; the PM tool still keeps you honest. In a group (up to three; four with instructor approval): assign an owner per area, not per week. One workable split: tools & MCP server (HOS 2), agents & orchestration (HOS 1 + HOS 4's team), traces & guards (HOS 3), and evaluation & demo (HOS 4's harness, plus owning the demo script). With three people, one person takes two areas. Everyone still commits code: in a group, the Git Workflow & Collaboration criterion looks for commits from every member, not one big push before the deadline. Solo, it looks for steady commits across the term.

Build: same Create → Evaluate habit, bigger scope

Rest of Week 9 → Week 10
  • Start from your HOS 4 team and harness. They're the skeleton. Change what the process needs; don't start over.
  • Scaffold with Antigravity, then judge it. Ask for a plan first (/plan) and review it before it builds. The AI Use Guide covers what to disclose. Scope what you ask it to build one story at a time, the same way each HOS's Create stage worked, and read every diff.
  • Get the happy path working first: one trigger → the whole process → a result, before widening any step.
  • Write eval scenarios early. A first draft of 15+ in Week 9, and check whether a run fits the free tier's rate limits. Use a smaller model for the simulated user and the judge.
  • Keep traces on. The demo needs one real failure trace from development: what broke, how it showed up, and what fixed it.
  • Rehearse in the simulators when you're unsure how a layer should behave: 1 · 2 · 3 · 4.

Standups

Async is fine

Brief, regular check-ins once building starts — a group chat is fine. Solo? Write the same three lines to yourself, or post them to your instructor. The point is to catch "I've been stuck for three days" before demo week, not during it.

Standup — [date] — [name]
Did:      closed "repeat-call guard on the review agent" (HOS 3)
Next:     first 5 eval scenarios for the brand reviewer (HOS 4)
Blocked:  a full eval run hits the free-tier limit — need to split it or use a smaller judge model

Minimum bar

Outcome O5

A project that meets the course outcome does all of this:

  • Runs end to end from a single trigger, with no human clicks between steps except justified checkpoints.
  • Has at least 3 custom tools, served over MCP.
  • Has at least 2 cooperating agents.
  • Has at least one self-correction mechanism, shown working in a real trace.
  • Has an eval suite of 15+ agent-on-agent scenarios reporting success rate, cost per task, and latency.
  • Has a README someone could read in 3 minutes: the problem, an architecture diagram, metrics, one failure and how it was fixed, and a link to the demo video.

Self-check and reflection

Feeds your Author Contribution statement

Self-check from the schedule: can you explain, without notes, how each of the 4 HOS shows up in your final project, where reasoning, planning, tool use and memory each live, and your success rate, cost per task and p95 latency? Say it out loud once before the demo.

Reflection prompts

  • Each member: what you personally built (link commits or issues), one decision you argued for, and one thing a teammate did that made the project better. Solo: what you built, one decision you'd make differently, and one piece of feedback (instructor or classmate) that changed the project.
  • Everyone: one moment where a trace or an eval caught a problem before it reached the demo.

Your final report ends with an Author Contribution statement detailing each member's weekly tasks (solo: your own weekly tasks); these notes are its raw material. Full description: the Term Project section of the syllabus (in Brightspace).
