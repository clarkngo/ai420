⚠ Illustrative example only. Campus Event Promoter, its college, its team and its numbers are made up, and the demo is a scripted HTML model: no real model, database or network calls. Your project doesn't have to look like this. It has to combine all four HOS to automate a real, multi-step process.

The project at a glance

Group of threeAll 4 HOS

The process. At a small, fictional college, one events coordinator promotes about ten events a month by hand: look up the event, see what worked before, write a post, check it against the style guide, and schedule it. Campus Event Promoter is a small autonomous marketing department that runs those steps from a single trigger. It's the course description's marketing example, cut to a size a team can finish.

Step in the processNow done byWhy
Look up the event and past postsAgent ResearcherWhat's relevant varies by event and audience.
Write the postAgent WriterIt takes judgment and changes every time.
Check it against the brand rules and the recordAgent Brand ReviewerA separate agent, because a writer is a poor judge of its own work.
Approve anything naming a speakerHuman checkpointA wrong name is a real harm to a real person. Only these posts stop for it.
Pick the time and schedule itDeterministic code"9 a.m. the Tuesday before" is a fixed rule. No agent needed.
HOSWhat it is in Campus Event PromoterLitmus test: why it's really that HOS
HOS 1 · loop & patternsA plan-and-execute coordinator built from ADK workflow agents, and a reflection LoopAgent between Writer and Reviewer.The team can say why each pattern fits its step, and the plan is real state a later step can change.
HOS 2 · tools, MCP & memoryget_event, search_past_posts and get_brand_rules on an MCP server, plus long-term memory of what worked per audience, kept in app: state in a SQLite session store.The tools reach the process's real data over MCP, not a hardcoded list. Memory changes a later run's output.
HOS 3 · traces & self-correctionA trace of every run (ADK callbacks to a file, and adk web's Trace view), a repeat-call guard, a two-round cap on review, and a hand-off when either trips.The guard exists because of a real failure trace, and that trace is in the demo.
HOS 4 · team & evaluationThree cooperating agents and a coordinator, with an eval of 18 agent-on-agent scenarios.The team is compared with a single agent on success, cost and latency, and the judge was checked by hand.

The team and who owned what

One owner per area, not per week, as the Roles section suggests. With three people, one person takes two areas.

    Three releases: what was in each

    Phase 1 · 2 · 3

    Campus Event Promoter didn't appear in Week 10. Each phase released a working system version and a report, the same as yours will.

    End of Week 3 · Phase 1

    System v0.3

    • Repo with a README, a .gitignore and no keys committed
    • One HOS 1 agent that turns a pasted event brief into a draft post, running from a fresh clone
    • A one-page proposal with 3 references: the process, the first map of its steps, which HOS goes where, and who owns what
    End of Week 7 · Phase 2

    System v0.7

    • The MCP server with its three tools, and memory across runs
    • Writer and Reviewer running from one trigger, with a trace recorded for every run
    • No guards yet, so a run can spin until the step limit. Listed honestly as a known issue.
    • A 3–4 page progress report with 12+ references
    End of Week 10 · Phase 3

    System v1.0

    • Repeat-call guard, review cap and hand-off, each from a failure they saw in a trace
    • Coordinator, three agents, the checkpoint and scheduling code, end to end
    • 18 scenarios with success, cost and latency, compared with a single agent
    • One-click Codespace launch and a README execution guide with an AI tool disclosure
    • Final report, presentation and demo video
    What they cut, and said so

    Posting to real social accounts (v1.0 writes to a schedule file) and support for events in two rooms, which is why 2 of the 18 scenarios still fail. Both are on the "what didn't make it" slide. That's the Integration Debate in practice: they cut features, not traces or the eval.

    What makes this a strong example

    Use it as a checklist
    • It starts from a real process. Every step in the map is justified as an agent, as code, or as a person, and not everything is an agent.
    • Every HOS is real, not a token mention. For each one the team can point to a specific part of the system and say why it's that HOS (the litmus test in the second table).
    • One run shows all four. The demo doesn't tour features one by one. A single trigger moves through the plan, the tools, the team and the guardrails, and the trace shows it.
    • It shows a real failure. The trace from Week 9 proves the guard earned its place, which is more convincing than a demo where nothing goes wrong.
    • Its numbers include the cost. The team wins on success and loses on cost and latency, and the presenters say both.
    • It's honest about what's missing. The cut features and the two failing scenarios are named, with what the team would do next.
    • Every member is visible. Owners per area, commits from all three, everyone speaks, and the title card names who built what.

    Now plan yours: map your process, write your backlog, then use the freeze checklist and the Demo plan.
